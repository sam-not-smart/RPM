WILLIAM A. BERNHARD AND ANDREW Z. PATRZALEK
Department ofBiophysics, University ofRochester, Rochester, New York 14642
BERNHARD, W. A., AND PATRZALEK, A. Z. ESR Characteristics of One-Electron Reduced
Thymine in Monomer, Oligomer, and Polymer Derivatives. Radiat. Res. 117, 379-394 (1989).
ESR spectra have been recorded of one-electron reduced thymine (T-) trapped in a variety
of thymine derivatives and matrices. Most of the data were taken at Q-band microwave fre-
quencies on 12 M LiCl glasses containing oligo(T) varying from 2 to 18 nucleotides in length. A
nonlinear least-squares simulation program was used to simulate the ESR spectra, resulting in
a parameter set that can be used to generate T- powder spectra at X- and Q-band frequencies.
Although it is not possible to identify a unique set of g and hyperfine tensors, it is possible to
establish boundaries for these interactions and propose a parameter set that is likely and reason-
able. As the trapping molecule is varied from TdR to oligo(T) to poly(T), there are small changes
in the interaction parameters. The changes are not large enough to account for the differences
between hfc values determined from DNA fiber data versus hyperfine coupling values measured
from monomer systems. The analysis also indicates that, in these systems, T- does not protonate
between 4 and 77 K at neutral pH. ? 1989 Academic Press, Inc.
INTRODUCTION
Two or more free radical products are trapped in duplex DNA when it is exposed
to the direct effects of ionizing radiation at temperatures of 77 K or below. One of
these radicals, the thymine radical anion (Structure I), has been identified with a high
degree of certainty. The most persuasive evidence comes from measurements that
demonstrate the conversion of an ESR doublet into the readily identifiable eight-line
spectrum of the 5,6-dihydrothymine-5-yl spectrum (1). This conversion, due to the
protonation of T- at C6, in conjunction with the doublet structure of the ESR spec-
trum (2-4) leaves little question that the T- radical is trapped in DNA. There are
questions, however, as to what fraction of the free radical population is assignable to
T-, as to whether T- protonates at OH prior to protonation at C6, and as to the
identity of the other free radicals trapped in DNA (5). To answer these questions
using ESR spectroscopy, it is important to determine what the characteristics of the
T- radical are when it is trapped in the environment of the DNA duplex.
Much of the pioneering work on radical ions produced in the nucleic bases was
done by Sevilla and co-workers (2-4). They studied electron attachment in glasses,
such as 8 M NaOD and 12 M LiC1, using photooxidation of K4Fe(CN)6 to generate
electrons. Others have employed ionizing radiation to generate radical anions of the
bases in various glasses (6-8). The ESR studies of T- in these systems indicate that
the major hyperfine coupling, A(H6), varies with conditions. A proton on 04 (see

In addition to the glassy systems, ESR measurements on T- have been made in
single crystals, aqueous solutions, and DNA fibers. Table I summarizes these results.
A precise measurement of the A(H6) tensor was obtained via an electron nuclear
double resonance (ENDOR) study of thymidine crystals (9). The isotropic hyperfine

estimated from DNA fiber data (11, 12). The fiber system has the advantage that
the T- radicals are partially aligned and the disadvantage that the T- spectrum is
superimposed on the spectrum of one or more other radicals. A best fit of the fiber
data is obtained using a free radical population consisting of 50% thymine radical
anion and 50% guanine radical cation (G'). There is some debate over this T-/G+
hypothesis (5, 13). One can see from Table I that the ESR interactions determined
from the DNA fiber system do not agree well with other systems. The difference in
Ai,,so(CH3) is particularly notable.
It would be advantageous in the analysis of DNA if the ESR spectrum of T- was
known more precisely. It is clear that the spectrum of T- varies with chemical envi-
ronment and substituents. We set out, therefore, to determine a set of parameters
that will generate the spectrum of T- trapped in duplex DNA. The strategy is first to
determine the ESR hyperfine and g interactions in T-, or at least better define the
boundaries of the interactions, and then to observe how these interactions are influ-
enced by environment and conformation. The method is to study T- generated in
12 M LiCl glasses containing various thymine derivatives. We will return to the re-
sults given in Table I in the Discussion.

ESR. Most of the data were collected at Q-band microwave frequencies using a Varian E- 12 spectrome-
ter. At Q band, X irradiation was done using a Machlett OEG 60 X-ray tube operating at 50 kV and 20
mA, giving 6 ,Gy/h at the sample position. Irradiation time was 15 min. The Dewar type cryostat used
for irradiation and ESR measurement is described elsewhere (14). The irradiation temperature was ~-2.5
K and ESR data were collected at 4 K. To avoid rapid passage effects, the sweep time was 30 min and the
modulation frequency was 1.0 kHz. The ~-20 mW microwave power source was attenuated - 50 db.
Because these settings give a very weak AFC signal, it was necessary to check for distortion of the spectra.
This was done by monitoring a reference, a ruby crystal mounted on the side wall of the cavity, with each
sweep. If the ruby signal deviated from proper symmetry, the spectrum was retaken.
Some data were collected at X-band microwave frequencies using an IBM/Bruker 200 DRC. The radia-
tion source and total dose were the same but the dose rate was - 30 gGy/h. The cryostat was a Joule-
Thompson-type refrigerator that provides an irradiation temperature of 11 K and measurement temper-
ature of 6 K. As with the Q band, low power and low frequency modulation were employed to avoid rapid
passage effects.
Simulations. ESR spectra were simulated using a nonlinear least-squares procedure described elsewhere
(15). This procedure fits between one and six ESR spectra simultaneously using a spin hamiltonian that
contains a g-tensor and up to seven hyperfine tensors. The tensors need not be coaxial. The linewidth is
treated as a tensor and is a peak-to-peak value of the first derivative of a Gaussian lineshape. The simula-
tions reported here utilized 890 orientations of the radical relative to the external magnetic field (Bo).
Background spectra were obtained using the sample preparations described above without the thymine
derivative. Background subtraction and baseline corrections were made as part of the nonlinear least-
squares procedure.
It is useful to define a quantity, R, that provides a measure of the goodness-of-fit while remaining insensi-
tive to spectral structure and intensity. We adopt for R the definition used for the residual index in X-ray
structure
R = 1Yoi - Ycil/2 Yoil, (1)
where Yoi are the data points making up the ESR spectrum and Yei are the calculated values. Typically,
when R < 0.05 the fit is very good. A fit obtained with an R = 0.01 is shown in Fig. 1.
When fitting the T- spectrum, indeed most powder spectra of organic radicals, there are numerous
parameter sets that will give low R values. The problem of multiple minima is a common one; the spectra
have insufficient structure to readily distinguish a global minimum in the least-squares differences. This
problem of multiple minima can be reduced, but not eliminated, by several features of the program. One
is to fit more than one spectrum at a time, particularly spectra taken at different microwave frequencies.
This reduces the number and range of local minima by introducing additional data that vary in a way
predicted by the spin hamiltonian. In the case ofT- the g and the A(H6) tensors both give rise to significant
variations in the Q-band spectrum versus the X-band spectrum. Another important feature is that each
parameter being varied under the least-squares procedure can be confined to a designated window. This
can be used to search only that portion of parameter space that is considered realistic.

The axes of the simulation have been fixed by forcing the off-diagonal elements of
A(H6) to zero. The x, y, z axes are defined as shown in Structure I. Since it is known
that gyy will be parallel to A(H6),,, it is also possible to set gx, and gy, equal to zero.
The W tensor is unconstrained.
The simulation parameters and R values for the spectra in Figs. 2 and 3 are given
in Table II. The R values, ranging from 0.01 to 0.04, indicate that the fits are very
good. The standard deviations given in parentheses reflect the variance of each pa-
rameter within the least-squares minimum. The absolute error of these parameters
may be significantly larger because of the problem with multiple minima. It is likely,
however, that these parameters are reasonable estimates of the actual interactions.
More important, variations in the ESR spectra are quantitatively reflected in changes
in the simulation parameters, making it possible to use comparisons to estimate weak
interactions.

The strategy behind using DNA packaged in a bundle of fibers is to enhance the
spectral information by aligning the DNA so that the bases are coplanar. With all the
bases coplanar, the spectrum taken with Bo perpendicular to the base plane will be of
single crystal quality and the spectrum with Bo parallel to the base plane will be due
to two-dimensional instead of three-dimensional tensors. In actuality the bases are
not entirely coplanar. It is necessary, in A-DNA, to introduce parameters to account
for base tilt relative to the helix axis and, in all cases, to account for regions where
DNA itself is not parallel to the fiber package axis (12). Thus the advantage of en-
hanced spectral structure is partially lost in the necessity of increasing the number of
variational parameters. In addition, the T- spectrum is always superimposed on the
spectrum of one or more other radicals. The task of simulating a composite spectrum,
consisting of two or more superimposed spectra, is very difficult. Analysis of the DNA
data would be facilitated if the spectral parameters of the T'- radical were known.

with those from the LiCl glass data shows the largest discrepancy is in Aiso,,(CH3). The
point that the CH3 hfc of 10-11 MHz is almost certainly too large has been made
before (5), but this is not the key issue. Simulation of the DNA fiber data is a multi-

If the parameters presented in Table III are to be useful in quantifying the T-
contribution in DNA, then it is important to determine to what degree, if any, the
parameters change when T- is located in DNA. Our analysis of the oligo(T) and
poly(T) data indicates that variations due to conformation and matrix are not large
enough to account for the differences in simulation parameters found for fiber DNA
versus LiCl glasses. An experiment that would shed some light on this would be to
simulate the DNA fiber data using the parameters in Table III. This, in conjunction
with a simulation of a spectrum of the same DNA but as a random powder instead
of as a fiber bundle, should help to resolve these discrepancies. Of particular interest
is the fiber simulation with B0 normal to the base planes. At this orientation the fiber
parameters predict a hyperfine pattern composed of a 36-MHz doublet (1:1), a 12-
MHz triplet (l:1:1), an 11-MHz quartet (1:3:3:1), and a 9-MHz doublet (1:1). The
pattern is approximately 10 lines with intensity ratios of 1:5:11:15:16:16:15:11:5:1
and a spectral width of 112 MHz. The splitting is large enough that this hyperfine
pattern must be simulating distinctive features of the DNA fiber spectrum. If the
Aiso(CH3) is too large, then these features must, at least in part, be due to other radical
products. Two possibilities, for example, are the N6 deprotonated cytosine cation
and the N2 deprotonated guanine cation.
Another important aspect of electron trapping by thymine in DNA is the proton-
ation state of the electron adduct. Since there is evidence that T- will readily proton-
ate at 04, it is important to be sure whether or not protonation at 04 can be discerned
with ESR. Differences in the spectra of thymine electron adducts under basic, neutral,
and acidic conditions have been noted (8). Since protonation at 04 causes measur-
able changes in the oligomer system, we conclude that, at neutral pH, the major
fraction of T- trapped in oligo(T) or poly(T) is not protonated at 04 in the 4 to 77K
temperature region. This observation is important to determining the factors which
govern the trapping sites. It means that, at temperatures below 78 K, protonation
does not play a key role in electron trapping at the thymine site and that T probably
retains its negative charge until protonation occurs at C6, as originally proposed (1).
Also, from the perspective of the opposing adenine base, in duplex DNA, the adenine
base remains neutral. This could be important because proton transfer to 04 of thy-
mine would result in a negative charge on the opposing adenine. That negative charge
will influence the distribution of the electron and hole trapping in its immediate vi-
cinity.

The results presented here redirect attention to the possibility that thymine is not
the only site of electron trapping at temperatures below 78 K. In the early DNA fiber
work (11), cytosine was maintained to be a possible trapping site along with thymine.
Sevilla et al. noted that C*- could not be readily distinguished from T- in their studies
of one-electron reduced dinucleoside phosphates (4). As we have stressed, both C-
and T- are well characterized by single crystal ESR, and there is no evidence as of
yet that would preclude cytosine as a site of electron trapping in DNA (5). We are
now in the process of studying one-electron reduced cytosine and oligomers of mixed
base composition. The preliminary findings are that at <78 K cytosine, not thymine,
is the dominant site of electron trapping (21). This correlates with recent measure-
ments showing that cytosine has a slightly larger reduction potential than thymine.l
It is feasible that electron trapping at low temperatures occurs at all the bases, with a
distribution governed by the reduction potentials and relative proton donor-acceptor
properties of the DNA base pairs.'
CONCLUSIONS
The thymine radical anion, T-, does not protonate in oligo(T) or poly(T) at tem-
peratures between 4 and 77 K. Changes in conformation and trapping environment
produce relatively minor changes in the T- ESR spectrum. In the LiCl glasses, and
by inference in the previously published DNA fiber system, it does not appear possi-
ble, using existing ESR data, to specify a unique set of hyperfine and g interactions.
It is possible, however, to determine boundaries for these interactions. A set of simu-
lation parameters that are likely and reasonable are presented that can be used to
simulate T- ESR spectra in samples containing randomly oriented radicals.
