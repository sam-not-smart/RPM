ABSTRACT
This thesis investigates radical pair magnetoreception using a fully quantum mechanical
description of two electron spins coupled to discrete nuclear environments. The model includes
Zeeman, anisotropic hyperfine, exchange, dipolar, and quadrupolar interactions, with open-system
effects introduced through a Gorini–Kossakowski–Sudarshan–Lindblad master equation. The
central aim is to determine when semiclassical and Markovian approximations fail and to identify
the role played by the nuclear environment in shaping spin dynamics, coherence, and quantum
correlations.
Numerical results show that biologically motivated Markovian dissipation is too weak to
significantly alter radical pair dynamics on microsecond timescales, while explicit quantum
treatment of the nuclei reveals structured oscillatory behavior absent in Overhauser-field
approximations. Tracking subsystem entropies demonstrates that the nuclear environment can act
as an internal non-Markovian bath, temporarily storing and returning quantum information to the
electronic subsystem. Localized nuclear control operations further show that strongly coupled
nuclei primarily redistribute spin populations, whereas weak near-Zeeman nuclei reshape the
spectral structure of the dynamics. A hypothetical quantum-information-theoretic model of
nuclear decay is also introduced, in which decay-generated entanglement induces a depolarizing
channel on the remaining proton and measurably restructures radical pair spin dynamics.
Taken together, these results support a picture of radical pair systems as controllable
mesoscopic quantum systems in which the nuclear environment is an active, structured subsystem
rather than a passive source of noise.

CHAPTER 1
INTRODUCTION
Radical pair systems provide a unique platform for studying quantum dynamics in complex,
room-temperature environments. Originally proposed as the underlying mechanism for avian
magnetoreception, the radical pair mechanism describes the coherent evolution of two electron
spins formed through photoinduced charge transfer, whose spin-dependent recombination
pathways are sensitive to weak magnetic fields. Despite operating in a biologically warm and
noisy environment, these systems exhibit coherent spin dynamics on microsecond timescales,
raising fundamental questions about the role of quantum effects in biological processes.
With strong supporting evidence that quantum mechanics is at play in these biological systems
[], this thesis seeks to test how quantum these systems might be. The appropriateness of certain
classical, semiclassical, and simplying assumptions, whether more complex dynamics than simple
spin-flipping are at play, and whether entanglement might have a non-trivial position within a
quantum mechanical model of this system.
Traditional approaches to modeling radical pair dynamics have largely relied on semiclassical
or phenomenological descriptions of the surrounding nuclear environment. In particular, the
Overhauser field approximation replaces the discrete nuclear spin bath with an effective classical
magnetic field, significantly simplifying the problem while capturing average magnetic effects.
Similarly, environmental interactions are often modeled using Markovian open-system
frameworks, in which decoherence and dissipation are assumed to erase memory of past system
states. While these approximations have proven useful in many contexts, they inherently neglect
quantum correlations, discrete environmental structure, and information backflow between
subsystems.
In recent years, there has been growing interest in understanding whether such approximations
are sufficient to describe radical pair systems operating in biologically relevant regimes. The
radical pair, together with its surrounding nuclear spins, constitutes a mesoscopic quantum system
1


--- PAGE 12 ---
in which multiple interaction scales—hyperfine, Zeeman, exchange, and dipolar
couplings—compete to shape the dynamics. In this regime, explicitly quantum mechanical
features such as entanglement generation, correlation transport, and non-Markovian memory
effects may play a significant role. Determining when these features become important, and how
they influence observable spin dynamics, is central to developing a more complete understanding
of the radical pair mechanism.
This thesis adopts a fully quantum mechanical framework to investigate radical pair dynamics
beyond semiclassical approximations. The system is modeled as a composite many-body spin
network consisting of two electron spins coupled to discrete nuclear environments, with dynamics
governed by a Hamiltonian including Zeeman, anisotropic hyperfine, exchange, dipolar, and
quadrupolar interactions. Open-system effects are incorporated through a
Gorini–Kossakowski–Sudarshan–Lindblad (GKSL) master equation, providing a controlled
baseline for environmental decoherence while enabling systematic exploration of its limitations.
A central objective of this work is to characterize how quantum correlations evolve within the
radical pair system and to determine the extent to which the nuclear environment acts as a passive
source of noise versus an active quantum subsystem. To this end, tools from quantum information
theory—such as von Neumann entropy, mutual information, and logarithmic negativity—are
employed alongside traditional observables like singlet and triplet populations. These measures
allow direct tracking of entanglement generation, redistribution, and revival within the system,
providing insight into the flow of quantum information between electrons and nuclei.
The results of this work demonstrate that the nuclear environment plays a far more active role
than is typically assumed in semiclassical models. Explicit quantum mechanical treatment of
nuclear spins reveals structured entanglement dynamics in which quantum information is
exchanged between electrons and nuclei over time. In particular, the nuclear environment can act
as an effective non-Markovian bath, temporarily storing quantum correlations and returning them
to the electronic subsystem, giving rise to coherent entanglement revivals. These effects emerge
even in the absence of an external environment, highlighting the intrinsic memory contained
2


--- PAGE 13 ---
within the finite nuclear spin system.
In addition to characterizing passive dynamics, this thesis explores the extent to which the
nuclear environment can be used to actively control radical pair behavior. Localized nuclear spin
operations, implemented as unitary rotations, are shown to induce significant modifications to
both population dynamics and spectral structure, with distinct roles played by strongly and weakly
coupled nuclei. These results demonstrate that different components of the nuclear environment
can act as control channels, selectively influencing either population redistribution or frequency
content of the dynamics.
Building on this perspective, a novel quantum-information-theoretic model of nuclear decay is
introduced. In this framework, a decay event is treated as an isometric mapping that conserves
total angular momentum while generating entanglement between the remaining proton and
emitted leptonic degrees of freedom. Tracing out the emitted particles induces a depolarizing
quantum channel on the nuclear spin, whose strength is directly linked to the degree of
entanglement generated during decay. Embedding this process within the radical pair model
reveals that even a single localized, non-unitary event can significantly restructure global spin
dynamics, with observable consequences in singlet–triplet populations.
Taken together, these results support a unified picture in which the nuclear environment is not
merely a source of decoherence, but a structured and dynamically relevant quantum subsystem.
The interplay between coherent spin interactions, environmental coupling, and internal nuclear
dynamics places radical pair systems in a regime where quantum information flow plays a central
role in determining observable behavior. This perspective suggests new avenues for understanding
and potentially controlling spin-dependent processes in biological and chemical systems.
The structure of this thesis is as follows. Chapter 2 introduces the quantum mechanical radical
pair model and its governing Hamiltonian. Chapter 3 extends this framework to include
open-system dynamics using the GKSL formalism. Chapter 4 defines the quantum correlation
measures used to analyze the system. Chapter 5 presents the quantum model of nuclear decay and
its embedding into the radical pair environment. Chapter 6 contains the main results, including

dynamics, controlled nuclear operations, and decay-induced effects. Finally, Chapters 7 and 8
discuss the broader implications of these findings and outline directions for future work.

CHAPTER 2
RADICAL PAIR MODEL + HAMILTONIAN
This chapter defines the quantum mechanical model used to describe radical pair spin
dynamics throughout this thesis. The radical pair is treated as a composite quantum system
consisting of two interacting electron spins coupled to local nuclear spin environments. This
model is extended in chapter 3 to include open-system dynamics. The formulation is quantum
theory-based with chemical and biological contexts determining parameter choices and modeling
assumptions, while the underlying dynamics are governed by a well-defined many-body
Hamiltonian [REF].
This approach allows radical pair dynamics to be analyzed using tools from quantum
information theory and open quantum systems, enabling the study of entanglement, correlations,
and memory effects beyond traditional yield-based descriptions [REF]. The Hamiltonian
introduced here provides the closed-system foundation to be extended in later chapters.

CHAPTER 3
OPEN QUANTUM SYSTEM FRAMEWORK
The Hamiltonian introduced in Chapter 2 describes the coherent spin dynamics of a radical
pair and its surrounding nuclear environment under unitary time evolution. While this
closed-system description is essential for identifying the intrinsic interaction mechanisms
governing singlet–triplet interconversion, it is insufficient for modeling realistic biological
systems.
In biological environments, radical pairs are embedded in complex protein scaffolds and
solvent environments that contain a vast number of additional degrees of freedom. These
environmental modes interact continuously with the electronic spins, leading to decoherence,
dissipation, and relaxation processes that cannot be captured by unitary dynamics alone. As a
result, a complete description of radical pair spin dynamics requires the framework of open
quantum systems, in which the system of interest is coupled to an external environment (or bath)
and evolves non-unitarily.
This chapter introduces the density matrix formalism and the
Gorini–Kossakowski–Sudarshan–Lindblad (GKSL) master equation used throughout this thesis as
a baseline to model open-system effects. The physical interpretation of the dominant decoherence
and relaxation mechanisms is discussed, along with the assumptions underlying Markovian bath
models and their limitations in biological contexts.

MARKOVIAN MASTER EQUATIONS
3.1 Born-Markov Approximation
To obtain a tractable equation of motion for 𝜌 𝑆 (𝑡), it is common to assume:
1. Weak system–bath coupling (Born approximation):
The interaction between the radical pair spins and the environment is sufficiently weak that
the bath remains near thermal equilibrium.
2. Short bath correlation time (Markov approximation):
Environmental correlation functions decay much faster than the intrinsic system dynamics,
allowing the bath to have no memory of past system states.
Under these assumptions, the reduced dynamics of the system become local in time and are
governed by a Markovian master equation.

3.3 3.4.1 Dephasing
Dephasing refers to the decay of quantum coherences without energy exchange between the
system and the environment. In the singlet–triplet basis, dephasing suppresses off-diagonal
density matrix elements while leaving populations unchanged.
Physically, dephasing arises from fluctuations in local magnetic fields generated by slow
nuclear motion, protein conformational dynamics, and solvent-induced magnetic noise. In radical
pair systems, dephasing limits the lifetime of coherent singlet–triplet oscillations, directly
impacting magnetic field sensitivity and entanglement.

3.4 3.4.2 Dissipation
Dissipation involves energy exchange between the system and its environment, leading to
population relaxation between eigenstates of the system Hamiltonian. In radical pairs, dissipation
can drive transitions between different spin eigenstates, redistributing population among singlet
and triplet manifolds.
Microscopically, dissipative processes originate from coupling to environmental modes that
can absorb or emit energy, such as vibrational degrees of freedom of the protein scaffold or
solvent phonons.
Dissipation is typically modeled using Lindblad operators that induce transitions between
system eigenstates and depends explicitly on the bath temperature and spectral density.

3.5 3.5 Interpretation of the Bosonic Bath
In this work, environmental effects are modeled using an effective bosonic bath. This bath

3.6 3.6 Limitations of Markovian Bath Models
While Markovian GKSL master equations are widely used and computationally efficient, they
impose strong physical assumptions that are not always justified in biological systems.
In particular, Markovian baths:
• Assume instantaneous loss of environmental memory,
• Suppress system–environment correlations,
• Cannot capture information backflow from the environment to the system,
• Predict monotonic decay of coherence and entanglement.
However, biological environments are often structured, featuring slow protein motions,
discrete vibrational modes, and heterogeneous nuclear spin distributions. These features can
introduce non-Markovian effects that significantly alter spin dynamics.

3.7 3.7 Transition to Non-Markovian Dynamics
Markovian baths provide a valuable baseline for understanding the dominant relaxation
mechanisms acting on radical pairs. However, they inherently erase memory effects and constrain
the dynamics to simple exponential decay.
A central theme of this thesis is that structured environments can qualitatively modify
radical pair dynamics, enabling non-Markovian behavior such as coherence revivals,
entanglement backflow, and enhanced sensitivity to environmental control.
In subsequent chapters, the Markovian assumption is progressively relaxed by introducing
structured and few-mode environments that interpolate between idealized GKSL dynamics and
strongly non-Markovian regimes. This transition enables systematic exploration of how
environmental structure influences coherence, entanglement, and information flow in radical pair
systems.

CHAPTER 4
QUANTIFYING QUANTUM CORRELATIONS
The radical pair mechanism has traditionally been characterized using chemically motivated
observables, most notably singlet and triplet reaction yields. While such quantities are essential
for connecting theory to experiment, they provide only a limited view of the underlying quantum
dynamics. In particular, yield-based observables are insensitive to the structure and distribution of
quantum correlations within the radical pair system and its surrounding nuclear environment.
In this thesis, the radical pair is treated as a composite open quantum system whose dynamics
naturally generate entanglement, classical correlations, and information flow between subsystems.
To characterize these effects, tools from quantum information theory are employed alongside
conventional spin-chemical observables. This chapter introduces the subsystem partitions and
correlation measures used throughout the remainder of the thesis and explains their physical
relevance in the context of radical pair dynamics.
The central objective of this chapter is to define what is measured and why these measurements
matter. By framing radical pair dynamics in quantum-information-theoretic language, this work
provides a bridge between spin chemistry and modern many-body quantum theory.

4.8 4.4.2 Why Log-Negativity is Essential
Log-negativity is the primary entanglement metric used in this thesis for several reasons:
Mixed-state robustness
Radical pair systems are inherently open and rapidly become mixed due to coupling with
nuclear and bosonic environments. Many entanglement measures are either undefined or
intractable for mixed states, whereas log-negativity remains well-defined and numerically stable.
Operational meaning
A nonzero logarithmic negativity certifies the presence of non-separable quantum
correlations. Its monotonicity under local operations and classical communication ensures that
observed entanglement cannot be generated artificially by subsystem coarse-graining.
Sensitivity to revivals
Log-negativity is particularly sensitive to non-Markovian dynamics. Temporary increases in
entanglement—often referred to as entanglement revivals—are clearly visible in this measure,
making it an ideal diagnostic for information backflow from structured environments.
These properties make log-negativity well suited for probing whether radical pair dynamics
exhibit genuinely quantum behavior beyond classical correlation models.

Having established the quantum mechanical radical pair model and its open-system extension

The radical pair is modeled as a composite many-body quantum system consisting of two

In realistic biological environments, however, the radical pair is not an isolated system. Protein

rapidly open-system dynamics drive the radical pair toward a steady state and quantify the
timescale over which the system retains memory of its initial condition. Second, we compare the
full quantum nuclear model with semiclassical approximations of the nuclear environment to
identify regimes where explicitly quantum nuclear dynamics become essential. Third, we examine
how competing interaction scales and environmental couplings influence the generation and
persistence of quantum correlations within the radical pair system.

5.14 6.1 Open-System Dynamics and Thermalization
Motivation
Radical pair reactions occur within complex biological environments containing many
uncontrolled degrees of freedom, including protein vibrations, solvent fluctuations, and
unresolved nuclear spins. As a result, the radical pair spin system cannot be considered perfectly
isolated and must, in principle, be treated as an open quantum system. While the Hamiltonian
dynamics introduced in Chapter 2 capture the intrinsic spin interactions responsible for
singlet–triplet interconversion, environmental couplings may introduce additional decoherence
and dissipative processes that modify the effective spin dynamics.
Beyond simple decoherence, environmental interactions may qualitatively alter system
behavior through quantum measurement effects. In particular, Kominis proposed that
spin-selective recombination channels in radical pair reactions effectively perform continuous
measurements of the singlet projector, potentially driving the system into a quantum Zeno regime
in which specific spin subspaces become dynamically stabilized. In this regime, sufficiently
strong environmental coupling suppresses coherent transitions and confines the system to
restricted regions of Hilbert space.
Motivated by this possibility, we investigate whether biologically inspired environmental
dissipation could produce analogous effects within a more general open-system description of the
radical pair. Specifically, we explore whether spontaneous-emission–type Lindblad processes
acting on the system’s energy eigenbasis could generate Zeno-like stabilization or protected
dynamical subspaces within the radical pair Hilbert space.
The simulations presented in this section therefore test whether environmentally induced
dissipation at biologically plausible rates can significantly influence radical pair dynamics on the

6.2 Overhauser Field Approximation vs Full Quantum Nuclear Dynamics
Many theoretical treatments of radical pair magnetoreception replace the explicit quantum
mechanical nuclear spin environment with an effective classical magnetic field acting on the
electron spins. This approach, commonly referred to as the Overhauser field approximation,
models the nuclear environment as a static or randomly sampled effective magnetic field
generated by the surrounding nuclei. Such approximations greatly reduce computational
complexity by eliminating the nuclear Hilbert space while still capturing the average magnetic
influence of the nuclear environment on the radical pair.
However, this semiclassical description neglects several intrinsically quantum mechanical
features of the nuclear spin environment. In particular, explicit nuclear spins can become
entangled with the electronic subsystem and can exchange quantum information with the radical
pair over time. These processes cannot be captured by a classical field approximation and may
therefore play an important role in determining the spin dynamics of the system.
To investigate the validity of the Overhauser approximation in the context of radical pair
magnetoreception, we compare the full quantum mechanical model of the nuclear environment
with a semiclassical Overhauser-field treatment. The objective of this comparison is to determine
whether the classical approximation faithfully reproduces the dynamical structure of the radical
pair system or whether important features arise from explicitly quantum nuclear degrees of
freedom.

6.3 Entanglement Dynamics and Exchange–Dipolar Interaction Effects
While the singlet–triplet populations of radical pair systems provide a direct observable for
magnetoreception, they capture only a limited portion of the underlying quantum dynamics. The
full radical pair system consists of a network of interacting spins in which quantum correlations
can flow between electrons and nuclei. Tracking these correlations provides insight into how

quantum information propagates through the system and whether the nuclear environment acts
merely as classical noise or as an active participant in the spin dynamics.
To investigate this behavior, the von Neumann entropy of various subsystems was tracked

entanglement generation and redistribution within the system. In particular, increases in the
entropy of a subsystem indicate the buildup of entanglement with the remainder of the system.
This analysis serves two purposes within the broader goal of validating the need for a full
quantum mechanical treatment of radical pair dynamics. First, it reveals whether the nuclear
environment behaves as a passive background field or as a dynamical quantum reservoir capable
of storing and returning quantum information. Second, it allows us to determine how additional
interaction terms, particularly exchange and dipolar couplings, influence the flow of entanglement
within the radical pair system.

5.33 Implications for Radical Pair Modeling
The results presented here reinforce the central conclusion of this section: that radical pair

In such regimes, semiclassical approximations that treat the nuclear environment as a static or
averaged magnetic field are unable to capture the full dynamical behavior of the system.
Explicit quantum mechanical treatment of the nuclear spins is therefore essential for
accurately describing:
• the redistribution of entanglement within the system,
• coherent information backflow between electrons and nuclei,
• the emergence of slow dynamical structures driven by the Zeeman interaction.
These findings further support the view that radical pairs should be understood as mesoscopic
quantum systems whose behavior cannot be reduced to simple classical noise models.

5.36 5.4.3 Interpretation and Physical Implications
The results of these controlled nuclear operations reveal a clear distinction between the roles
played by different components of the nuclear environment. These distinct behaviors are
illustrated directly in Figs. 5.7 and 5.8, which show that different nuclei within the same
environment can control qualitatively different aspects of the radical pair dynamics.
Strongly coupled nuclei exert a dominant influence on the electronic spin populations, enabling
direct redistribution of singlet and triplet occupation through local operations. In contrast, weak
nuclei with hyperfine strengths near the Zeeman scale primarily influence the spectral structure of
the dynamics, redistributing frequency components without inducing large population changes.
This separation of roles highlights the multi-scale nature of the radical pair system. Different
interaction strengths govern different aspects of the dynamics:
• strong hyperfine couplings control fast oscillatory behavior and population transfer,

--- PAGE 64 ---
• Zeeman-scale interactions set the global dynamical envelope,
• weak, near-resonant nuclei mediate coupling between these regimes.
Importantly, these results reinforce the conclusion of Section 5.3 that the nuclear environment
cannot be treated as a passive background field. Instead, it functions as a structured quantum
subsystem whose individual components can selectively control different features of the dynamics.
From a broader perspective, this suggests that control of radical pair dynamics may not require
direct manipulation of the electron spins themselves. Instead, targeted interventions in the nuclear
environment—particularly those tuned to relevant interaction scales—may provide a viable
pathway for steering spin dynamics and, consequently, spin-dependent chemical processes.
These findings provide further evidence that radical pair systems should be understood as
controllable mesoscopic quantum systems, in which environmental degrees of freedom can be
harnessed to engineer both population dynamics and spectral structure.

CHAPTER 6
RESULTS
Having established the quantum mechanical radical pair model and its open-system extension
in the preceding chapters, we now investigate the dynamical behavior of the system through
numerical simulation. The objective of this results chapter is to determine how the interplay of
coherent spin interactions and environmental coupling shapes the evolution of quantum
correlations, information flow, and observable spin dynamics within the radical pair mechanism.
The radical pair is modeled as a composite many-body quantum system consisting of two
electron spins coupled to surrounding nuclear environments through anisotropic hyperfine
interactions. As discussed in Chapter 2, the coherent dynamics of this system arise from a
Hamiltonian containing Zeeman, hyperfine, exchange, dipolar, and quadrupolar terms. These
interactions generate singlet–triplet interconversion, electron–nuclear entanglement, and complex
multi-spin dynamics that cannot generally be captured by semiclassical approximations.
In realistic biological environments, however, the radical pair is not an isolated system. Protein
vibrations, solvent fluctuations, and unresolved environmental degrees of freedom introduce
decoherence and relaxation processes that can modify spin dynamics. Chapter 3 introduced the
Gorini–Kossakowski–Sudarshan–Lindblad (GKSL) master equation as a baseline framework for
incorporating such environmental effects in a controlled manner. Within this framework,
dissipative and dephasing processes are modeled through Lindblad jump operators that couple the
radical pair to an effective bosonic bath representing its surrounding molecular environment.
The results presented in this chapter pursue three central objectives. First, we investigate how
rapidly open-system dynamics drive the radical pair toward a steady state and quantify the
timescale over which the system retains memory of its initial condition. Second, we compare the
full quantum nuclear model with semiclassical approximations of the nuclear environment to
identify regimes where explicitly quantum nuclear dynamics become essential. Third, we examine
60


--- PAGE 71 ---
how competing interaction scales and environmental couplings influence the generation and
persistence of quantum correlations within the radical pair system.
Together, these investigations establish the dynamical regimes in which a fully quantum
mechanical treatment of the radical pair is necessary and identify the physical mechanisms
responsible for long-lived coherence, correlation exchange, and environmental memory effects.
OPEN-SYSTEM DYNAMICS AND THERMALIZATION
Motivation. Radical pair reactions occur within complex biological environments containing
many uncontrolled degrees of freedom, including protein vibrations, solvent fluctuations, and
unresolved nuclear spins. As a result, the radical pair spin system cannot be considered perfectly
isolated and must, in principle, be treated as an open quantum system. While the Hamiltonian
dynamics introduced in Chapter 2 capture the intrinsic spin interactions responsible for
singlet–triplet interconversion, environmental couplings may introduce additional decoherence
and dissipative processes that modify the effective spin dynamics.
Beyond simple decoherence, environmental interactions may qualitatively alter system
behavior through quantum measurement effects. In particular, Kominis proposed that
spin-selective recombination channels in radical pair reactions effectively perform continuous
measurements of the singlet projector, potentially driving the system into a quantum Zeno regime
in which specific spin subspaces become dynamically stabilized. In this regime, sufficiently
strong environmental coupling suppresses coherent transitions and confines the system to
restricted regions of Hilbert space.
Motivated by this possibility, we investigate whether biologically inspired environmental
dissipation could produce analogous effects within a more general open-system description of the
radical pair. Specifically, we explore whether spontaneous-emission–type Lindblad processes
acting on the system’s energy eigenbasis could generate Zeno-like stabilization or protected
dynamical subspaces within the radical pair Hilbert space.

CHAPTER 7
DISCUSSION
7.1 7.1 Overview of Findings
The results presented in Chapter 6 collectively demonstrate that radical pair systems, when
treated within a fully quantum mechanical framework, exhibit rich and structured dynamics that
are not captured by conventional semiclassical or Markovian descriptions. Across all
investigations—thermalization, semiclassical comparisons, entanglement tracking, controlled
nuclear operations, and nuclear decay—a consistent picture emerges: the nuclear environment
plays a central and active role in shaping the spin dynamics of the system.
In particular, three key themes arise from this work:
1. The nuclear environment acts as a finite, structured quantum subsystem rather than a
featureless bath.
2. Quantum correlations between electrons and nuclei give rise to non-Markovian behavior
and information backflow.
3. Localized operations and processes within the nuclear environment can be used to actively
steer both population dynamics and spectral structure.
These themes unify the diverse results of Chapter 6 into a coherent physical interpretation of
the radical pair system as a controllable mesoscopic quantum network.
7.2 7.2 Limitations of Semiclassical and Markovian Descriptions
A central motivation of this work was to assess the validity of commonly used approximations
in radical pair modeling, particularly the Overhauser field approximation and Markovian
open-system treatments.
79


--- PAGE 90 ---
The comparison between the full quantum model and the Overhauser approximation (Section
6.2) demonstrates that replacing the discrete nuclear environment with a classical field fails to
capture key dynamical features. In particular, the semiclassical model suppresses coherent
oscillatory structure and eliminates the possibility of entanglement between electrons and nuclei.
While such approximations may reproduce average magnetic effects, they obscure the underlying
quantum dynamics that govern information flow within the system.
Similarly, the open-system analysis (Section 6.1) reveals that Markovian decoherence
processes inspired by spontaneous emission, when parameterized using biologically relevant
rates, have a negligible impact on the microsecond timescales relevant to radical pair dynamics.
This suggests that environmental decoherence alone cannot account for the observed loss or
redistribution of coherence within the system. Instead, the dominant mechanisms arise from
internal interactions within the finite nuclear environment.
Taken together, these findings indicate that both semiclassical and Markovian approaches,
while computationally convenient, are insufficient for capturing the full dynamical richness of
radical pair systems. A fully quantum mechanical treatment is necessary to resolve the interplay
between interaction scales, correlations, and memory effects.
7.3 7.3 The Nuclear Environment as a Non-Markovian Quantum Bath
One of the most significant outcomes of this work is the identification of the nuclear
environment as an effective non-Markovian bath.
In contrast to traditional open-system models, where the environment acts as an infinite
reservoir that irreversibly absorbs information, the finite nuclear spin system retains and
redistributes quantum correlations over time. This is most clearly observed in the entanglement
dynamics (Section 6.3), where correlations between electrons and nuclei exhibit oscillatory
behavior and partial revivals.
These revivals indicate that quantum information initially transferred from the electronic
subsystem to the nuclei is not lost, but rather temporarily stored and later returned. This behavior
80


--- PAGE 91 ---
is a hallmark of non-Markovian dynamics and arises naturally from the discrete and finite
structure of the nuclear environment.
Importantly, this non-Markovian behavior does not require an explicitly engineered
environment. It emerges intrinsically from the hyperfine-coupled spin network. The presence of
multiple interaction scales—particularly the interplay between Zeeman and hyperfine
couplings—leads to structured beating patterns that govern the flow of correlations.
This perspective reframes the role of the nuclear environment: rather than acting as a source
of decoherence, it functions as a dynamical memory medium that mediates information
exchange within the system.
This paper also extends the potential idea of zeno stabilization being responsible for the
unintuitively-long coherence times expected in the radical pair electrons. Previous work [REF]
hypothesizes zeno stabilization as an avenue for these coherence times and research [REF] has
shown such phenomena is more basic models. The nuclei could serve as a highly entangled
subsystem, whose continuous observation could stabilize the electron’s coherence. This is
theoretical and further studies need to be conducted before anything conclusive could be asserted.
7.4 7.4 Interaction Scales and Dynamical Structure
The results further highlight the importance of multiple competing interaction scales in
determining radical pair dynamics.
Hyperfine interactions govern fast, local electron–nuclear dynamics, while the Zeeman
interaction sets a slower global timescale associated with the external magnetic field. Exchange
and dipolar interactions introduce additional structure, modifying the coupling between electronic
spin states and producing envelope modulations in observables such as entanglement.
The analysis of entanglement beating (Section 6.3) reveals that these envelope modulations are
closely linked to the Zeeman frequency, indicating that even weak interactions can play a
dominant role in shaping long-time dynamics. This interplay between strong and weak couplings
leads to a hierarchy of dynamical features, with different interaction scales controlling different
81


--- PAGE 92 ---
aspects of the evolution.
This multi-scale structure is essential for understanding how small perturbations—such as
weak magnetic fields—can produce measurable effects in radical pair systems. It also underpins
the sensitivity of the system to targeted manipulations of specific nuclear spins.
7.5 7.5 Nuclear Spins as Control Channels
A key extension of the analysis in this work is the exploration of active control of radical pair
dynamics through localized nuclear operations (Section 6.4).
The 𝜋-pulse experiments reveal a clear separation of roles within the nuclear environment:
• Strongly coupled nuclei act as population control channels, enabling direct redistribution
of singlet and triplet populations.
• Weak, near-Zeeman nuclei act as spectral control channels, modifying the frequency
content of the dynamics without significantly altering population averages.
This distinction reflects the different ways in which nuclei couple to the electronic subsystem.
Strong hyperfine interactions directly influence the dominant terms in the Hamiltonian, while
weaker interactions modulate the structure of the dynamical spectrum.
The ability to selectively target these different regimes suggests that the nuclear environment
provides multiple independent “handles” for steering system behavior. Importantly, this control is
achieved without direct manipulation of the electron spins themselves, highlighting the potential
of the environment as a resource for quantum control.
7.6 7.6 Nuclear Decay as a Quantum Channel
The introduction of a quantum-information-theoretic model of nuclear decay (Chapter 5 and
Section 6.5) further extends the role of the nuclear environment beyond coherent control.
In this framework, a decay event is treated as a localized quantum channel whose strength is
determined by the degree of entanglement generated between the remaining proton and emitted
82


--- PAGE 93 ---
leptons. The resulting depolarizing map introduces a controlled loss of information in the nuclear
spin.
The simulations demonstrate that even a single decay event can significantly alter the global
dynamics of the radical pair system. As the entanglement parameter increases, the decay induces
progressively stronger suppression of coherent oscillations and redistribution of spin populations.
These results illustrate that quantum processes external to the radical pair system can
influence its dynamics through their effect on the nuclear environment. At the same time, the
persistence of structured dynamics following the decay indicates that the remaining nuclei
continue to support coherent behavior.
An important open question concerns the physical magnitude of the entanglement generated
during such decay processes. In this work, the entanglement parameter is treated as a free
variable, allowing systematic exploration of its effects. The results suggest that observable
quantities within the radical pair system—particularly singlet and triplet populations—may serve
as indirect probes of this otherwise inaccessible entanglement.
7.7 Implications for Radical Pair Mechanisms and Quantum Biology
The findings of this thesis have several implications for the broader study of radical pair
systems and quantum effects in biological environments.
First, they suggest that quantum coherence and entanglement may persist and play a functional
role over biologically relevant timescales, supported by the structured dynamics of the nuclear
environment. This challenges the assumption that environmental noise rapidly destroys quantum
effects in such systems.
Second, the identification of the nuclear environment as a non-Markovian subsystem highlights
the importance of considering finite, structured environments in models of biological quantum
systems. Simplified treatments that neglect this structure may miss key dynamical features.
Third, the demonstration of control through nuclear operations raises the possibility that
biological systems could, in principle, exploit internal degrees of freedom to regulate spin
dynamics. While the specific control protocols considered here are theoretical, they provide a

--- PAGE 94 ---
framework for thinking about how environmental structure could be harnessed in more realistic
settings.
Finally, the decay model illustrates how processes outside the immediate radical pair system
can influence its behavior through entanglement and information transfer. This points toward a
broader view of radical pairs as components of larger quantum networks embedded within
complex environments.
7.7 7.8 Limitations and Future Directions
While this work provides a comprehensive quantum mechanical treatment of radical pair
dynamics, several limitations should be noted.
The nuclear environments considered here are relatively small, consisting of a limited number
of spins with simplified hyperfine tensors. Extending the model to include larger and more
realistic nuclear spin networks would provide a more accurate representation of biological
systems.
The open-system treatment is restricted to Markovian Lindblad dynamics. While this serves as
a useful baseline, incorporating explicitly non-Markovian environmental models—such as
structured or few-mode baths—would allow more direct comparison with the intrinsic
non-Markovian behavior observed in the nuclear environment.
The nuclear decay model is intentionally simplified, neglecting orbital angular momentum,
energy distributions, and detailed weak-interaction physics. A more complete treatment of decay
processes would be required to assess their physical relevance in realistic systems.
Finally, experimental validation of these results remains an open challenge. Direct
measurement of nuclear spin dynamics and electron–nuclear entanglement in radical pair systems
is difficult, and indirect probes—such as spin-selective reaction yields—must be carefully
interpreted.
Despite these limitations, the framework developed in this thesis provides a foundation for
future studies of quantum dynamics in complex spin systems.

7.8 7.9 Concluding Remarks
The central conclusion of this work is that the nuclear environment in radical pair systems is
not a passive background, but a structured and dynamically active quantum subsystem.
Through its ability to store, redistribute, and mediate quantum correlations, the nuclear
environment plays a decisive role in shaping both the population dynamics and spectral structure
of the system. Moreover, it provides multiple pathways for control, ranging from coherent spin
rotations to irreversible quantum channels.
This perspective shifts the focus of radical pair modeling from the electrons alone to the full
electron–nuclear system, emphasizing the importance of quantum information flow in
understanding and potentially exploiting these systems.
In this sense, radical pairs represent not only a model for magnetoreception, but also a broader
platform for exploring quantum dynamics in mesoscopic, environmentally embedded systems. -
